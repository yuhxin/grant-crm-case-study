A
Exhibit A

A Fragmented View

Every important record of our grantee partners lives on Submittable, the platform TMT uses to manage grants.

However, different grant teams work with grantees independently, creating the issue of multiple (incomplete, even different) sources of information on the same organisation. Even a single proposal rarely survives one draft!

The grant process is also SUPER long: calls, coffee chats and feedback changes the grantee's informational input over time, creating more complex information flows.


Ultimately, finding the full picture means clicking through entry after entry on Submittable, hoping you've found all of them, rather than reading one record.

B
Exhibit B

Lost
information

Not all important information is formal. The phone call, the coffee chat, the comment made to one person on the team but never shared with the rest are all equally important. Philanthropy is relational in a way application forms are not built to capture.

C
Exhibit C

Watching a foundation
try to fix it

What I appreciated was that TMT treated this as a real structural problem instead of a minor inconvenience. Scoping a new pilot system took multiple high‑level meetings across different teams, because they understood that no tech fix will help with this.
A fix meant agreeing on what to track, by whom, and how much of a relationship to formalise without making partners feel surveilled.

I wasn't part of those design discussions, but I wanted to be. Watching from the outside is what shaped the proposal below.

D
The proposal

A partner‑centric
model

The fix isn't fewer records, as teams still need their own fund‑level entries. What would work, on a systemic level, is a shared identity that every entry resolves back to, plus a place for the conversation that never made it into any form.

Fig. D2. Before and after, in four questions
QuestionCurrent systemProposed system
Full history in one search?No, manual reconstruction needed Yes, one partner/record
Is informal context captured?NoYes, via a lightweight log
Who can see the full picture?Whoever pieces it togetherAnyone on the team
Is discretion built in?Left to individual judgementExplicit visibility rules
Principle 01

One identity, many entries

Fund‑level records can stay separate where useful, but every entry should resolve back to one underlying partner, so history is a query, not a reconstruction.

Principle 02

Logging has to be easier than remembering

A two‑line, timestamped note after a call is enough to stop relationship context from living only in one person's memory.

Principle 03

Governance decided in advance

Not every personal conversation should become an internal file. Consent and visibility need explicit rules, not in‑the‑moment judgement calls.

E
Closing note

Why I keep coming
back to this

The third principle above is one I've thought about from the other side too. When I've fundraised in a personal capacity, I was the one deciding what to share with someone raising money on my behalf, and what to keep private. That taught me documentation systems in philanthropy aren't only a technical problem! They're a question of trust, consent, and judgement about what belongs on the record.

This pattern, I think, is close to a structural default for any organisation running multiple funds through multiple teams without a unifying data model. This is exactly why I'm drawn to roles that sit at the intersection of grants operations, data governance, and systems design.
