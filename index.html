Case file no. 002 — Grants operations

Fragmented grant knowledge, and how I'd fix it

A field study from my internship at The Majurity Trust: where one grant relationship quietly turns into four disconnected records, where the most important context never gets written down at all, and what a partner‑centric system would look like instead.

Exhibit A

One relationship, four records

Grant teams at TMT worked with NGOs independently, under different funding pots. A single proposal rarely survives one draft either: calls and feedback turn it into a new attempt rather than an edit to the same file. Stack teams, funds, and attempts on top of each other, and one real relationship stops looking like one thing at all.

Answering "what is our full history with this organisation" meant knowing which teams had touched them, which funds, and how many attempts each went through — then reconstructing it by hand, usually from memory.

Exhibit B

The layer that never got written down

Alongside the formal record sat a second, invisible one: the phone call, the coffee chat, the comment made to one person on the team but never shared with the rest. Philanthropy is relational in a way application forms are not built to capture.

Exhibit C

Watching a foundation try to fix it

TMT treated this as a real structural problem, not a minor inconvenience. Scoping a new pilot system took multiple high‑level meetings across different groups, because the fix wasn't a software purchase — it meant agreeing on what to track, by whom, and how much of a relationship to formalise without making partners feel surveilled.

I wasn't part of those design discussions, but I wanted to be. Watching from the outside is what shaped the proposal below.

The proposal

A partner‑centric model

The fix isn't fewer records — teams still need their own fund‑level entries. The fix is a shared identity that every entry resolves back to, plus a place for the conversation that never made it into any form.

Fig. D2 — Before and after, in four questions
QuestionCurrent systemProposed system
Full history in one search?No — manual reconstructionYes — one partner record
Is informal context captured?NoYes, via a lightweight log
Who can see the full picture?Whoever pieces it togetherAnyone on the team
Is discretion built in?Left to individual judgementExplicit visibility rules
Principle 01

One identity, many entries

Fund‑level records can stay separate where useful, but every entry should resolve back to one underlying partner, so history is a query, not a reconstruction.

Principle 02

Logging has to be easier than remembering

A two‑line, timestamped note after a call is enough to stop relationship context from living only in one person's memory.

Principle 03

Governance decided in advance

Not every personal conversation should become an internal file. Consent and visibility need explicit rules, not in‑the‑moment judgement calls.

Closing note

Why I keep coming back to this

The third principle above is one I've thought about from the other side too. When I've fundraised in a personal capacity, I was the one deciding what to share with someone raising money on my behalf, and what to keep private. That taught me documentation systems in philanthropy aren't only a technical problem — they're a question of trust, consent, and judgement about what belongs on the record.

This pattern isn't unique to one foundation. It's close to a structural default for any organisation running multiple funds through multiple teams without a unifying data model — which is exactly why I'm drawn to roles that sit at the intersection of grants operations, data governance, and systems design.
